A map before a deep dive

Your project sits at the intersection of game AI, agent-based economics, operations research and social simulation. Reading across those fields should be more useful than searching for one “better agent algorithm.”

This is a browsing map: skim the concepts, follow whatever catches your interest, and leave the technical papers until you want depth. The connections to Learning Lord are my interpretation, not claims made by the authors.

From the implementation and design notes, I see three particularly useful questions:

  • Which possibilities do citizens consider? Your planner constructs a restricted set of alternatives. Better scoring cannot select a useful plan that was never generated.
  • What makes an outcome valuable? Needs, wealth, reserves, future food benefits and household interests interact. Those choices define the citizens’ motivations.
  • What happens when everyone follows those rules? Individually reasonable decisions can still produce shortages, inactivity, inequality or unstable prices.

The current implementation combines goal construction, forward prediction and wellbeing scoring; it also contains policies for provisioning, production and selling. Broader relationship ambitions and employment proposals extend beyond what is currently implemented.

Repository anchors: planner · prediction scoring · relationship objectives · deferred employment design

If you only open five things initially

  1. Epstein’s Why Model? — the purpose of a simulation.
  2. The Game AI Pro behavior-selection overview — individual behavior.
  3. CORE’s introductory economics text — markets and economic choice.
  4. MIT’s inventory lectures — practical production and provisioning.
  5. Ostrom’s lecture — collective organization.

01 / 15 Concepts 1–6

Model purpose and emergence

These help decide what “improvement” means before adding more machinery.

Concept Meaning and relevance here
01 Agent-based modeling Represent individuals and their interactions, then observe the society they produce. This is already your basic approach.
02 Generative explanation Ask whether plausible local rules can generate a particular social pattern, such as specialization or inequality.
03 Emergence Town-level behavior can appear without any citizen intending it. A shortage need not have a single responsible agent.
04 Heterogeneity Differences in resources, preferences, skills and information can matter more than making every citizen smarter.
05 Descriptive versus normative models Modeling how people behave differs from designing agents that make economically optimal choices.
06 Minimal models A deliberately small economy can reveal a mechanism more clearly than a richly detailed town.

Start: Joshua Epstein, Why Model? — a short, accessible essay about the many purposes of simulation.

Explore: Epstein and Axtell, Growing Artificial Societies — a book-length example of building social phenomena from interacting agents. Leigh Tesfatsion’s agent-based computational economics reading map is a useful directory when a particular economic topic catches your attention.

For your project, the immediate lesson is to distinguish “interesting behavior,” “believable behavior” and “evidence about real societies.” Each needs a different standard of success.

02 / 15 Concepts 7–12

Needs, preferences and utility

This is especially relevant because wellbeing is the common currency of your decisions.

Concept Meaning and relevance here
07 Utility functions A numerical representation of preferences. The formula determines what citizens are willing to sacrifice for what.
08 Diminishing marginal utility The next coin or loaf may matter less than the previous one. This changes accumulation and consumption incentives.
09 Homeostasis Behavior regulates internal conditions such as hunger and thirst around acceptable ranges.
10 Anticipatory regulation Prepare before a need becomes urgent: fill the pantry before hunger, rather than merely react afterward.
11 Multiple objectives and constraints Survival, comfort, wealth and obligations can be traded off—or some can be protected by explicit limits.
12 Hysteresis and inertia Require a meaningful improvement before switching activities, avoiding constant changes of mind.

Start: David “Rez” Graham, An Introduction to Utility Theory — practical game-AI discussion of scoring, response curves, selection and inertia.

Explore: Keramati and Gutkin, Homeostatic reinforcement learning for integrating reward collection and physiological stability — a technical account connecting internal needs to reward-seeking behavior.

A question worth keeping beside these readings: is wealth itself satisfying, or valuable because of the future options it buys? Your answer substantially changes the model.

03 / 15 Concepts 13–18

Ways to organize decisions

These are alternatives and combinations, not successive upgrades.

Concept Meaning and relevance here
13 Finite-state machines Explicit modes such as working, shopping and resting; straightforward when transitions remain manageable.
14 Behavior trees Compose priorities, conditions and actions into a reactive hierarchy. Useful for dependable routines.
15 Goal-oriented action planning Construct action sequences from prerequisites and effects. Closely related to your resource-acquisition search.
16 Hierarchical task networks Decompose “provision household” into known methods, reducing the amount of open-ended search.
17 Belief–desire–intention architectures Separate what an agent believes, wants and has committed to doing.
18 Hybrid architectures Combine routines, emergency reactions and deliberative planning, giving each a suitable job.

Start: Dawe and colleagues, Behavior Selection Algorithms: An Overview — a useful comparison before choosing an architecture.

Explore: Rao and Georgeff, BDI Agents: From Theory to Practice. For a more practical hierarchy example, Troy Humphreys’ Exploring HTN Planners through Example is available in the Game AI Pro collection.

My strongest simplification candidate here is routines for repeated maintenance, with planning reserved for consequential choices. That is a design option to evaluate, not a recommendation to replace your planner immediately.

04 / 15 Concepts 19–24

Time, planning horizons and delayed benefits

Your production questions connect directly to these concepts.

Concept Meaning and relevance here
19 Receding-horizon control Predict ahead, execute part of the plan, then reconsider using the actual state.
20 Terminal value Estimate what the state at the prediction boundary is worth beyond that boundary.
21 Discounted versus average reward Different ways to compare benefits over time; they can favor different behavior.
22 Temporal abstraction Treat a shopping trip or production job as a meaningful activity rather than many isolated steps.
23 Semi-Markov decision processes A framework for decisions whose actions take different amounts of time.
24 Reward shaping Add guidance to help decision-making; poorly chosen guidance can change the objective itself.

Start conceptually: Rawlings, Mayne and Diehl, Model Predictive Control: Theory, Computation, and Design — skim the introduction and finite-horizon discussion; the mathematics gets substantial.

Explore: Sutton, Precup and Singh, Between MDPs and semi-MDPs, on temporally extended actions; Ng, Harada and Russell, Policy invariance under reward transformations, on when reward shaping preserves an objective.

For Learning Lord, these readings illuminate the difference between giving inputs credit for future usefulness and paying the citizen for work that has not happened. The shaping theorem does not automatically apply to your current scoring scheme.

05 / 15 Concepts 25–30

Uncertainty, knowledge and learning

Prediction and learning are separate capabilities.

Concept Meaning and relevance here
25 Partial observability Citizens act on what they know, which may differ from the complete simulation state.
26 Belief updating Revise expectations after observing sales, shortages or another citizen’s behavior.
27 Risk-sensitive choice Reliable food supply may be preferable to a higher average return with a starvation risk.
28 Exploration versus exploitation Trying an uncertain product or price can be worthwhile because it teaches something.
29 Model-based versus model-free learning Learn a world model for planning, or learn useful behavior directly from experience.
30 Monte Carlo tree search Sample possible continuations when enumerating every future is too expensive.

Start: Sutton and Barto, Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction — chapters 1–3 for the vocabulary, chapter 8 for planning and learning. Reading these does not commit you to neural networks.

Explore: Poole and Mackworth’s Planning with Uncertainty, and Browne and colleagues’ A Survey of Monte Carlo Tree Search Methods.

Your seller trials already make exploration relevant. A useful distinction is whether a trial’s value comes only from immediate receipts or also from what it reveals about demand.

06 / 15 Concepts 31–36

Bounded rationality and behavioral economics

For believable people, consider the limits and shortcuts of decision-making.

Concept Meaning and relevance here
31 Bounded rationality Decisions are limited by knowledge, time and computational capacity.
32 Satisficing Stop searching when an option is good enough, rather than always finding the maximum.
33 Aspiration levels “Enough” can depend on experience and expectations, allowing standards to change.
34 Habits and routines Reuse successful behavior until circumstances give a reason to reconsider it.
35 Reference dependence and loss aversion Outcomes can feel good or bad relative to an expected state, rather than absolute wealth.
36 Framing and judgment heuristics The presentation of a choice and simple mental shortcuts can influence decisions.

Start: Herbert Simon, Rational Decision-Making in Business Organizations — particularly relevant to your preference for simple, general rules.

Explore: Daniel Kahneman, Maps of Bounded Rationality, on intuitive judgment and choice.

I would read Simon before adding psychological biases. Limited but understandable competence is a stronger starting point than making agents irrational in assorted ways.

07 / 15 Concepts 37–42

Economic choice and valuation

These help disentangle several issues currently grouped under profitability.

Concept Meaning and relevance here
37 Opportunity cost The cost of an action includes the best alternative it prevents, including other uses of time.
38 Comparative advantage Specialization depends on relative opportunity costs, not simply who is fastest at each task.
39 Marginal analysis Decide whether the next batch is worthwhile; average profitability may answer a different question.
40 Sunk costs Past, unrecoverable expenditure should not alone determine what to do next.
41 Relevant value versus accounting value Historical cost, replacement cost, sale proceeds and personal usefulness answer different questions.
42 Make-or-buy decisions Compare complete acquisition routes, including travel, capacity and alternative uses of labor.

Start: OpenStax’s overview of scarcity and opportunity cost and comparative advantage.

Explore: Its managerial-accounting sections on relevant information and make-or-buy decisions.

My main takeaway for your design: one price does not have to serve every purpose. Using separate concepts can simplify reasoning even when it adds a little data.

08 / 15 Concepts 43–48

Markets and exchange

A collection of profitable recipes does not automatically become a functioning economy.

Concept Meaning and relevance here
43 Reservation prices A buyer’s maximum willingness to pay and a seller’s minimum acceptable price need not match.
44 Price discovery Prices develop from offers, trades and learning; an asking price is not evidence of a completed sale.
45 Market clearing and rationing When supply and demand differ, prices are only one mechanism; availability and allocation also matter.
46 Search and transaction costs Finding a supplier, traveling and arranging exchange can make a cheap good expensive to acquire.
47 Market power A sole miller or indispensable supplier can influence terms differently from one of many competitors.
48 Matching and auction mechanisms Rules determine which buyer meets which seller and who obtains scarce stock.

Start: CORE Econ, The Economy 2.0 — browse the microeconomics units on firms, customers, supply and demand, and market failures.

Explore: Easley and Kleinberg’s chapters on auctions and matching markets in Networks, Crowds, and Markets.

For your town, first-come purchasing, supplier locations and limited stock are already economic institutions. They deserve as much attention as the price-update formula.

09 / 15 Concepts 49–54

Production, inventory and logistics

This may offer the most immediately useful reading for your recent problems.

Concept Meaning and relevance here
49 Reorder-point policies Replenish when stock reaches a threshold, accounting for how long replacement takes.
50 Safety stock Hold a buffer against uncertain demand or supply rather than targeting only average requirements.
51 Economic order quantity and lot sizing Balance frequent small purchases against larger purchases that tie up resources.
52 Setup costs and batching Travel and preparation can make several batches together preferable to isolated production.
53 Bottlenecks and capacity Output depends on constrained workers, equipment or slots, not merely available ingredients.
54 Bullwhip effects Delayed information and replenishment reactions can amplify small demand changes up a supply chain.

Start: MIT’s Logistics Systems lecture notes, particularly inventory management and safety stock.

Explore interactively: John Sterman’s Beer Distribution Game material, which introduces supply-chain behavior through a deceptively simple setting.

This literature gives you principled alternatives to choosing “two hours” or “twelve hours” of ingredients by intuition. It also explains why an individually sensible restocking policy can destabilize suppliers.

10 / 15 Concepts 55–60

Organizations and institutions

Your households, guilds and proposed businesses lead into these concepts.

Concept Meaning and relevance here
55 Household production A household coordinates consumption, unpaid work and shared resources—not just a pooled bank account.
56 Transaction-cost economics Some activities are easier to coordinate inside an organization than through repeated market transactions.
57 Principal–agent problems An employer’s objectives and an employee’s objectives differ, especially when effort is hard to observe.
58 Collective action and free riding Members benefit from shared work while potentially preferring someone else to perform it.
59 Commons governance Shared resources can be managed through local rules, monitoring and sanctions.
60 Polycentric governance Households, guilds and town authorities can govern overlapping activities at different scales.

Start: Elinor Ostrom, Beyond Markets and States — a particularly good match for your interest in collectives.

Explore: Ronald Coase’s Nobel lecture, and CORE’s discussion of firms, owners, managers and employees.

A useful challenge to your design: household allegiance, employment and guild membership need not be variations of the same relationship weight. They grant different rights and create different obligations.

11 / 15 Concepts 61–66

Relationships, cooperation and social networks

Separate different social mechanisms before combining them.

Concept Meaning and relevance here
61 Repeated games and reciprocity Future interaction can make cooperation worthwhile even when immediate selfishness would pay.
62 Trust and reputation Personal experience and reports from others affect willingness to trade, lend or cooperate.
63 Social preferences Agents may care directly about others’ outcomes, fairness or group success.
64 Homophily and network formation Similar people tend to connect; those connections can reinforce similarity.
65 Strong and weak ties Close relationships provide support; weaker connections can introduce new information and opportunities.
66 Diffusion and social learning Practices, information and expectations spread through contact rather than appearing everywhere simultaneously.

Start: Easley and Kleinberg, Networks, Crowds, and Markets — browse chapters 3–6 and the later chapters on information cascades and diffusion. The authors provide a free draft and individual chapters.

Explore a game implementation: McCoy and colleagues, Comme il Faut: A System for Authoring Playable Social Models, which represents reusable social norms and interactions.

Your relationship notes already ask about cooperation strengthening attachment and exploitation weakening it. This area offers vocabulary for distinguishing liking someone, trusting them, owing them and identifying with their group.

12 / 15 Concepts 67–72

System dynamics and economic stability

Zoom out from individual choices to the behavior of the whole town.

Concept Meaning and relevance here
67 Stocks and flows Inventory is a stock; production and consumption are flows. Confusing them produces misleading diagnostics.
68 Reinforcing and balancing feedback Some loops amplify change; others resist it. Both can operate simultaneously.
69 Delays and oscillation Slow production and price responses can create recurring shortages and gluts.
70 Path dependence Early events can push otherwise similar towns onto persistently different trajectories.
71 Liquidity constraints and coordination failures Useful work can remain undone because participants cannot finance inputs or coordinate complementary actions.
72 Resilience and redundancy Spare capacity and multiple suppliers may look inefficient until a disruption occurs.

Start: Donella Meadows, Leverage Points: Places to Intervene in a System — accessible and useful when deciding whether to adjust a number or change a rule.

Explore: MIT’s Introduction to System Dynamics and Tesfatsion’s economic modeling research areas.

For Learning Lord, this suggests observing distributions and time series: inventories, unmet needs, productive hours, cash availability and supplier dependence. A single citizen’s winning plan cannot explain the whole economy.

13 / 15 Concepts 73–78

Space, ecology and resource constraints

Physical and spatial constraints can create meaningful economic decisions.

Concept Meaning and relevance here
73 Spatial accessibility Goods can exist in the town yet be practically inaccessible because of distance or permissions.
74 Congestion and queues Shared workshops and services introduce waiting, which changes the value of otherwise attractive choices.
75 Renewable-resource dynamics Fishing and foraging can depend on replenishing stocks rather than unlimited action opportunities.
76 Carrying capacity An environment can support only so much sustained consumption under particular technologies and rules.
77 Common-pool depletion Individually beneficial extraction can undermine the resource everyone depends on.
78 Seasonality and disturbance Predictable cycles and occasional shocks create reasons for storage, diversification and cooperation.

Start by experimenting: The NetLogo Models Library offers small, inspectable models. Try Wolf Sheep Predation for resource feedback, plus the traffic and segregation models for spatial interactions.

Explore a game perspective: Tarn Adams, Simulation Principles from Dwarf Fortress.

I would treat these as possible sources of meaningful constraints. Add them when they create decisions you want, rather than because more physical detail automatically seems better.

14 / 15 Concepts 79–84

Character, narrative and understandable behavior

For interesting citizens and player experience, consider how behavior becomes legible.

Concept Meaning and relevance here
79 Character intentionality Actions feel coherent when an observer can understand the goal behind them.
80 Persistent personality Stable differences in priorities and habits create recognizable individuals.
81 Episodic memory and relationships Specific past events can explain present behavior more clearly than an unexplained relationship score.
82 Social practices and roles Reusable situations—hosting, bargaining, apologizing—create structure without scripting every encounter.
83 Emergent narrative and drama management Stories can arise from simulation, or a separate system can influence pacing and opportunities.
84 Generative agents Language-based memory, reflection and planning offer another approach to believable social behavior.

Start: Mateas and McCoy, An Architecture for Character-Rich Social Simulation.

Explore: Riedl and Young, Narrative Planning: Balancing Plot and Character, and Park and colleagues, Generative Agents: Interactive Simulacra of Human Behavior.

The latter is worth knowing about, but I would not infer that you should put language models inside your economy. Its memory-and-reflection ideas are interesting independently; believable conversation does not establish sound economic behavior.

15 / 15 Concepts 85–90

Evaluation and scientific discipline

These help tell improvements from attractive anecdotes.

Concept Meaning and relevance here
85 Verification versus validation Correctly implementing rules differs from showing that those rules represent the intended phenomenon.
86 Pattern-oriented modeling Evaluate several patterns together, rather than tuning until one chosen citizen behaves as desired.
87 Sensitivity analysis Discover which parameters meaningfully change outcomes and where behavior changes abruptly.
88 Structural uncertainty and equifinality Different rules can generate similar visible outcomes; matching one result does not identify its cause.
89 Controlled simulation experiments Compare interventions across matched starting states and repeated runs; isolate which mechanism changes behavior.
90 Scheduling and reproducibility Update order, randomness and simultaneous-action rules can affect results and must be explicit.

Start: Grimm and colleagues, The ODD Protocol for Describing Agent-Based and Other Simulation Models. Its categories help separate purpose, entities, scheduling, assumptions and observation.

Explore: ten Broeke, van Voorn and Ligtenberg, Which Sensitivity Analysis Method Should I Use for My Agent-Based Model?.

Your existing saved-state comparisons and decision diagnostics are a good foundation. The next conceptual step is testing whether an explanation survives changes in starting wealth, supply, population and timing.

A suggested reading route

  1. Epstein + the behavior-selection overview: establish what you want the model to accomplish and recognize the available architectures. Model purpose · Decision architectures
  2. CORE economics + inventory management: build vocabulary for the production and trade problems already present. Markets · Inventory
  3. Simon + the time-horizon material: consider how much reasoning citizens actually need, and how to account for consequences beyond their immediate plan. Bounded rationality · Planning horizons
  4. Ostrom + social networks: develop households, guilds, cooperation and institutions on clearer foundations. Institutions · Social networks
  5. Narrative research + simulation evaluation: make behavior understandable while checking that appealing stories are supported by the mechanics. Narrative · Evaluation

The biggest opportunity I see is separating motivations, practical routines, economic accounting and prediction. Your current wellbeing score is carrying parts of all four. Reading these fields should help you decide which responsibilities belong together—and where a simpler rule would produce clearer, more convincing behavior.
